Close Read: "I Will Fight No More Forever" — October 5, 1877

After the Battle of Little Bighorn, the U.S. Army pursued Native resistance across the Plains. One of the final holdouts was Chief Joseph of the Nez Perce, who led roughly 750 of his people on a 1,170-mile flight toward Canada, hoping to escape onto Sitting Bull's land — but was stopped just 40 miles short of the border.

Chief Joseph (Heinmot Tooyalakekt), photographed in 1903 by Edward S. Curtis. National Portrait Gallery, Smithsonian Institution. Public domain.
Map of the Nez Perce flight and key battle sites of 1877. U.S. Forest Service, 2004. Public domain.
"My people, some of them, have run away to the hills, and have no blankets, no food; no one knows where they are — perhaps freezing to death. I want to have time to look for my children, and see how many of them I can find. Maybe I shall find them among the dead. Hear me, my chiefs! I am tired; my heart is sick and sad. From where the sun now stands, I will fight no more forever."

Whose words are these, really?

Chief Joseph spoke only Nez Perce. His words were passed through at least one interpreter, then written down by Lieutenant Charles Erskine Scott Wood — a U.S. Army officer who later became a published poet. Historians still argue over how much of this speech is Joseph's own meaning and how much is Wood's literary polish. No version in Joseph's own hand exists.

What Happened to the Land

After resistance ended, the government's main tool changed — from war to a different kind of pressure. Reservation land that had been held in common by a tribe was broken into individual plots ("allotments"), and whatever land was left over was declared "surplus" and opened to white settlers.

Green = individual Native-held allotments  |  Pink = "surplus" land sold to settlers, checkerboarding the reservation

Document 2: "Kill the Indian, Save the Man" — Carlisle, 1879–1918

1
Military-style uniforms replaced tribal clothing.
2
Hair was cut short — for many Plains cultures, long hair carried deep spiritual and personal meaning.
3
Students were forbidden to speak their own languages — English only, everywhere on campus.
Pupils at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School, Pennsylvania, c. 1900. Public domain. Click the numbered dots.

Carlisle was founded in 1879 by Richard Henry Pratt, a former Army officer. In 1892, responding to a general who had said "the only good Indian is a dead one," Pratt offered what he believed was the humane alternative:

"In a sense, I agree with the sentiment, but only in this: that all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man."

One student: Tom Torlino

Tom Torlino, a young Navajo man, arrived at Carlisle on October 21, 1882. The school's photographer took his portrait that day — and again three and a half years later, just before Torlino left in August 1886.

Tom Torlino, Navajo. Photographs by John N. Choate, Carlisle Indian Industrial School, 1882 and 1885. Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center / National Archives. Public domain.

Pratt commissioned photos like these to send to Washington officials and donors, as proof his methods worked.

Closing: Wounded Knee, December 29, 1890

U.S. soldiers bury the dead in a mass grave after the Wounded Knee Massacre. Photo by Trager & Kuhn, January 1, 1891. Library of Congress, public domain.

At Wounded Knee Creek, South Dakota, U.S. troops killed an estimated 250–300 Lakota men, women, and children — the last major armed confrontation between the U.S. Army and Native nations on the Plains. It happened 14 years after Little Bighorn, and just three years after Carlisle's model had already spread to dozens of schools.
